When the Law Promises Education but the Classroom Delivers Colonialism

May 22, 2026

A scholar-practitioner's reading of the CBA Child Rights Toolkit on Education

The Canadian Bar Association’s Child Rights Toolkit on Education is a careful, thorough document. It maps CRC Articles 28 and 29, walks through federal and provincial legislation, cites landmark Supreme Court decisions, and ends with practice essentials for lawyers advocating on behalf of children. It is a useful inventory.

It is also, in important ways, a document that describes the architecture of a system without naming what that system does to certain children.

I want to sit with that gap.

What the Law Says

CRC Article 28 guarantees the right to education. Article 29 goes further: education shall be directed to the development of a child’s personality, talents, and abilities to their fullest potential and to the development of respect for the child’s own cultural identity, language, and values.

The CBA Toolkit notes that while Article 28 focuses on access, Article 29 specifies quality. The distinction matters. A child can be enrolled and still be uneducated in the sense Article 29 intends.

Canadian constitutional law distributes education across provincial jurisdictions, with federal authority retained specifically over “Indians” under section 91(24) of the Constitution Act, 1867. Jordan’s Principle, TRC Calls to Action 6 to 12, and UNDRIP Article 14 are all listed in the Toolkit. The legal architecture of Indigenous children’s right to education is, on paper, extensive.

And yet.

What Thirty Years in Classrooms Taught Me

My doctoral research is an autoethnographic study of my professional practice as a science educator across Ontario, Australia, and the Cordillera region of the Philippines. I spent many years in classrooms, labs, faculties of education, and postsecondary Indigenous institutions. What I built over that time, and what the dissertation documents, is an onto-ethico-epistemological (OEE) framework: a way of understanding how knowledge, ethics, and being intersect when an Indigenous educator tries to teach inside a colonial system.

The framework did not begin as theory. It began as a problem.

The problem was this: I was using the curriculum I was required to use in accredited schools, with Growing Success and Ontario Science and Technology guidelines. I was meeting every formal standard the system set. And I was watching Indigenous students, including students in the Ifugao region, students in James Bay communities, and students in Southern Ontario, navigate classrooms where their knowledge systems were either invisible or decorative. Where science meant positivism. Where the land was content, not the teacher.

The Toolkit’s language around Article 29 calls for respect for the child’s cultural identity, language, and values. What I documented across thirty years is that settler schooling regularly produces the opposite, not because individual teachers are malicious, but because the structural epistemology of Western science education is organized to render Indigenous knowledge either supplemental or absorbed.

That is not access failure. It is a quality failure. It is an Article 29 failure. And it is largely invisible in legal discourse because the child is enrolled, the school is accredited, and the curriculum has been approved.

The Jurisdictional Displacement of Indigenous Children’s Education Rights

The CBA Toolkit correctly identifies that education for Indigenous children often falls under federal rather than provincial jurisdiction. It lists the Indian Act sections 114 to 122, the Mi’kmaq Education Act, the First Nations Jurisdiction Over Education in British Columbia Act, Jordan’s Principle, and UNDRIP.

What it cannot fully capture, in a legal toolkit format, is the lived experience of that jurisdictional fracture.

In my research, I describe what I call the “ecology of urgency”: the condition of working as an Indigenous educator within systems whose structural logic consistently pushes back against Indigenous knowledge. When I was an administrator at an Indigenous postsecondary institution, the language of accreditation, budget allocation, and mainstream compliance reconfigured even Indigenous education spaces around Western institutional metrics. The institution was Indigenous in name and mandate. Its reporting structures, credit frameworks, and performance expectations belonged to the settler academy.

This is not an argument against accreditation or funding. It is an observation about what happens when the legal guarantee of Indigenous education rights is implemented through settler institutional structures. The right exists on paper. Its conditions of realization are colonial.

Jordan’s Principle was designed to ensure First Nations children access the services they need, when they need them, without getting caught in federal-provincial jurisdictional disputes. It is an important legal tool. What it does not, and cannot, resolve is the epistemological dispute inside the classroom: whose knowledge counts, whose science is legitimate, whose way of relating to land and ancestors is educationally valid.

Article 29 as Structural Challenge, Not Just Legal Standard

CRC Article 29(1)(c) states that education shall be directed to the development of respect for the child’s parents, cultural identity, language, and values. Article 29(1)(d) extends this to preparation for responsible life in a free society in the spirit of friendship among all peoples and persons of indigenous origin.

These are not vague aspirations. They are substantive standards. And they are, by my reading, routinely unmet for Indigenous children in Canadian classrooms, not because teachers lack goodwill, but because the curriculum and its underlying epistemology were not designed with Indigenous knowledge as a co-equal framework for understanding the natural world.

My OEE framework argues that decolonizing science education is not about adding Indigenous content to a Western curriculum. It is about recognizing that knowledge, ethics, and being are inseparable and that a science education that strips away the relational, spiritual, and communal dimensions of Indigenous knowing is not providing education in the Article 29 sense. It is providing something else: a credential, a compliance, and a processing through a system that was designed for a different body.

The Medicine Wheel as methodological architecture, the Four Directions as organizing principles for inquiry, and the body as researcher: these are not ornaments I added to a Western framework. They are the framework. And the system, in most of its accredited forms, does not recognize them as such.

What Lawyers Advocating for Indigenous Children Should Know

The CBA Toolkit offers strong practice guidance. Advocate explicitly for the child’s right to be heard. Make best-interest arguments specific and documented. Exhaust administrative remedies before escalating. Be aware that ongoing relationships between families and schools are affected by the tenor of litigation.

I would add, “Be aware of what the law cannot see.”

A child who is enrolled, attending, not formally disciplined, and receiving passing marks can still be experiencing a systematic denial of their right to an education directed at their cultural identity, language, and values. The legal harm is real; it is just not visible through enrollment data or academic records.

If you are advocating for Indigenous children in education matters, ask these questions. What language is instruction delivered in, and whose relationship to knowledge does that language assume? What does the school’s science curriculum treat as authoritative knowledge? Does the classroom practice engage with local Indigenous knowledge keepers, and if so, is that engagement genuine or ornamental? What is the school’s institutional relationship to the land it sits on?

These are not soft questions. They are Article 29 questions.

What I Carry Forward

The dissertation ends in the North quadrant of the Medicine Wheel, which in the tradition I have learned represents the reflection and synthesis of faith, trust, and faithfulness in relationships. I ended there because decolonization, as I have practiced and documented it across thirty years, is not a policy outcome. It is a recursive, relational, ongoing commitment to staying oriented toward Indigenous knowledge as the center, not the supplement.

The CBA Toolkit is a valuable resource. It is most valuable if practitioners use it not just as a checklist of legal standards but as a provocation: what would it mean to actually fulfill Article 29 for every Indigenous child in Canada? What structural, epistemological, and institutional changes would that require?

The answer is not more legislation, though legislation matters. The answer is a transformation of what we mean when we say education.

Until we meet again, all my relations.
