What the Algorithm Cannot See

April 7, 2026

James Baldwin once said the camera isn’t a neutral witness. It doesn’t just record, it learns what to look for. Built inside its lenses and joints is someone’s training, someone’s taste, someone’s blind spots. Yet we keep trusting that tiny glass eye as though truth sits behind it, waiting to be captured. We forget that the camera only ever points where it’s been taught to point.

That’s the same illusion we live with now in classrooms wired for artificial intelligence. We’re told these systems mirror “human intelligence.” But if the camera carries its maker’s gaze, then training data is the new lens, the curriculum of the machine. It’s not a clean record of the world; it’s a filtered memory. English-heavy, textbook-fluent, trimmed of everything messy or oral or local. What’s missing isn’t decoration. It’s foundation.

I think I learned this before algorithms even entered school. Back in 2004, teaching a class in Southeastern Ontario, I was handed a unit titled Strong and Stable Structures. The textbook had that familiar lab activity: toothpicks, glue, neat little bridges. Something measurable, tidy, closed off, the kind of thing that marks and grades well.

My students didn’t bring toothpicks. They walked in with sinew and hides, wood from the forest still smelling of rain. For a moment, I thought they’d misunderstood the task. Turns out, I had. They weren’t making models, they were making structures, the real thing, drawn from generations who had built shelters under those clouds and against that wind. They knew tensile strength the way you know the bend of your own elbow.

But none of that fit the rubric. The system couldn’t see what it hadn’t been trained to see.

Years later, I’ve watched students program robots to trace the geometric patterns of wampum belts. The code runs fine, the machine hums, but to the algorithm, it’s just JavaScript looping cleanly through a set of commands. It misses the point entirely, that those patterns carry law, memory, relationship. All invisible to the system math. And when you feed their work to today’s AI grader, it happens again, only faster. Anything that falls outside its dataset doesn’t just confuse it; it disappears.

That’s the quiet harm. Today’s “AI tutors” replicate the old colonial curriculum in digital form, slicker, faster, still blind. When a student answers from knowledge tied to land or tradition, the algorithm politely corrects them. It calls it an error, and rewards whatever it can measure.

This isn’t an accident or a glitch. It’s the structure. Someone decided what counts as data, what fits the box. Venture capital has no patience for slow things, or complicated ones. The worldview baked into most training sets treats relationships as inefficiencies, emotion as noise, and context as something to be scraped away.

And that’s the heart of it. The question isn’t just who designed it. It’s who gets to decide what the machine should ever know. Even “consultation” rings hollow when nobody has power to say no. True participation begins there, with refusal. Some knowledge doesn’t belong in datasets. Not because it’s secret, but because once you strip it from land, from duty, from kinship, it stops breathing. It becomes something studied, not lived.

Most talk of AI ethics stays at the checklist level, bias audits, fairness scores, procedural tidy-ups. But those don’t reach the actual bones of the thing. Nobody asks whether the machine, as built, should even be here, in these classrooms, with these students.

After thirty years teaching, I know learning doesn’t happen alone. It happens in circles, among land, people, ancestors, those yet to come. It’s never just “personal,” so it can’t be “personalized.” Algorithms trade in isolation; education thrives in relation.

That’s why inclusion, as framed today, doesn’t cut it. Adding every Indigenous word or story into the corpus isn’t progress if the act itself vandalizes meaning. Extraction dressed up as equity is still extraction. The real answer is refusal, a conscious, careful boundary.

Refusal isn’t technophobia. It’s sovereignty. It’s the simple respect of knowing some truths can’t survive translation into code. It’s standing guard over what must remain unseen if it’s to remain alive.

We choose the student, not the algorithm.
We choose the land, not the interface.
And we protect, fiercely, quietly, forever, the truths the machine was never built to recognize.
