Living in Two Worlds

April 10, 2026

What Education Still Gets Wrong About Indigenous Knowledge

“To be Indigenous is to live in two worlds.”

That line has followed me for decades. Not as a poetic idea, but as something I wake up inside every day.

One world is built on relationships: family, land, memory, language. Knowledge there isn’t owned or delivered. It grows through experience, through story, through the slow work of responsibility.

The other world is built on systems. Knowledge there is packaged, measured, credentialed, and standardized. It fits neatly into rubrics and policy documents. It’s efficient, yes—but often strangely disconnected from the world beyond the classroom.

Education keeps asking us to move between these worlds, but it rarely pauses to ask what gets lost in the crossing.

The Problem Isn’t Inclusion, It’s Translation

I’ve worked as a teacher, a teacher educator, and a researcher. Every year, I see more schools trying to include Indigenous perspectives.

There are curriculum units, guest speakers, land acknowledgements, professional development sessions—all of them offered in good faith.

But something doesn’t fully translate.

The issue isn’t that Indigenous knowledge is missing. It’s that the deeper assumptions of Western education still define what counts as “real” knowledge: how it’s verified, how it’s taught, and how it’s assessed.

Even when Indigenous content is present, it’s often simplified or aestheticized. It becomes safe.

And when that happens, it loses the relationships that give it life.

The Hidden Curriculum of Good Intentions

There’s always another message running beneath the official one.

Teachers talk about inclusion, respect, and diversity. But the structures they work within often teach something else entirely:

  • What can’t be measured doesn’t count.

  • Expertise comes from texts, not relationships.

  • Learning is personal, not collective.

Students notice. Especially those who live between worlds.

The message lands quietly but clearly: your way of knowing exists, but not here—not fully.

The Uncomfortable Work of Self-Reflection

When people talk about decolonizing education, they often mean curriculum reform. Swap in new resources, update the readings, revise the outcomes.

But the deeper work is internal. It asks questions that unsettle:

Whose knowledge feels “natural” in this space?
What do we accept as evidence?
When are we performing understanding instead of living it?
Are we inviting Indigenous voices—or borrowing them?

That kind of reflection shakes your sense of expertise. It doesn’t feel good, but growth rarely does.

Without it, though, decolonization becomes another layer of performance.

Beyond the Binary

This isn’t about rejecting Western education or romanticizing Indigenous knowledge. Real life doesn’t divide that cleanly.

Most of us move through both worlds every day. We carry blended histories, languages, and worldviews. The question isn’t which world to choose. It’s how to move between them honestly.

Maybe that shift looks like this:
from mastery to humility,
from delivery to dialogue,
from content to relationship.

It sounds small, but it changes everything.

What Education Could Become

If we took this seriously, classrooms would feel different.

They’d be less about control and more about co-creation.
Less about covering material and more about uncovering meaning.
Less about authority and more about shared responsibility.

Instead of asking, “What should students know?”
we’d start asking, “How are we accountable to each other while learning?”

That small shift could change the entire rhythm of education.

A Final Thought

Living in two worlds isn’t a problem to fix. It’s simply reality.

The challenge is that education still struggles to see that.

Until it does, it will keep recreating the same divisions it claims to resolve.
