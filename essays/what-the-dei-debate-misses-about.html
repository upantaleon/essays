What the DEI Debate Misses About Creative Resistance

June 4, 2026

There is a particular exhaustion that comes from watching a debate that keeps missing the point.

The current conversation about DEI rollbacks, in the United States and increasingly in Canada, tends to organize itself around a single axis: access versus restriction. Either institutions are making space for equity-focused work or they are dismantling it. Either creative expression that centers marginalized communities is being platformed or it is being suppressed. The assumption embedded in both positions is that the institutional frame is the relevant one, that what matters is what the institution does or fails to do.

This framing misses what decolonial creative practice actually is and what it actually does.

I want to try to say what it misses and why that gap is not incidental. It is structural. The DEI framework, even at its most expansive, was never built to hold what Indigenous creative practice carries. And the current backlash against DEI, for all its viciousness, is attacking a container while the water has long since moved.

Let me start with a specific memory.

I was teaching a unit on states of matter to a cohort of science teacher candidates. The lesson plan was approved. The curriculum alignment was documented. I was working, as I almost always was, inside the framework of Ontario’s Science and Technology curriculum, navigating its “big ideas” and “scope and sequence” while trying to find where Indigenous ways of knowing could actually enter, not as illustration, not as supplementary cultural content, but as epistemologically valid.

I used hula.

Not as metaphor. Not as warm-up activity. I had teacher candidates embody the movement of atoms and compounds through physical states, using the movement principles of hula, where the relationship between the dancer’s body, the land, and the ancestors is not decorative but constitutive of the knowledge being transmitted. The lesson worked precisely because it proposed something the curriculum guide could not contain: that the body moving in relationship to land and lineage is a way of knowing, not a way of performing what has already been known elsewhere by other means.

The institution approved the lesson plan. I want to sit with that for a moment, because it matters.

Institutional approval is not neutral. When a settler-based institution approves an Indigenous creative practice as pedagogical content, it is engaging in a specific kind of transaction. It is saying: this is legible to us, this fits our categories of acceptable innovation, this can be assessed, documented, and included in a portfolio of best practices. The practice is permitted to exist inside the institution, but on the institution’s terms of recognition.

This is not the same as the practice being understood. It is not the same as the institution being changed by it.

The question I kept returning to across thirty years of teaching was not whether I could get Indigenous content into the curriculum. Often I could. The question was whether the framework through which that content was received had been altered at all. Whether the epistemological ground had shifted. Whether my learners, after encountering hula as a way of knowing states of matter, understood science differently, or whether they understood themselves as having had a culturally enriching experience within science as they had always understood it.

These are not the same outcome. And the distinction between them is exactly what the DEI debate cannot hold.

The onto-ethico-epistemological framework I developed across my doctoral research, grounded in the Medicine Wheel and Four Directions teachings shared with me by Anishinaabe communities on Manitoulin Island, insists on a specific claim: ontology, ethics, and epistemology cannot be separated. How we know is inseparable from who we are being and what we are accountable to. This is not a philosophical preference. It is a structural description of how Indigenous knowledge actually works.

What this means for creative resistance is consequential. Creative work that operates only at the epistemological level, that says “here is a different way of representing or expressing this,” while leaving the ontological and ethical ground intact, is not resistance. It is diversity. It is the kind of content DEI frameworks are designed to include and manage.

Creative work that operates at all three levels simultaneously, that proposes a different way of being in relation to land, ancestors, and community as the very condition for a different kind of knowing, cannot be institutionally managed in the same way. It can be tolerated, approved, even celebrated. But it exceeds the categories available for managing it.

This is why the current DEI rollback, for all the real harm it is causing, is not attacking what it thinks it is attacking. It is attacking the institutional apparatus of inclusion, which was always a partial and managed accommodation of something more radical. The creative practices that carry decolonial knowledge are not housed in that apparatus. They predate it and will survive it.

I want to be careful here not to turn this into a triumphalist argument. The harm of DEI rollbacks is material and immediate. When funding for Indigenous education initiatives is cut, when equity offices are dismantled, when curricula are purged of content that names colonialism, real people lose access to resources, protection, and recognition they need. The institution failing to hold Indigenous creative practice on its own terms is different from the institution actively working to suppress it. Both are failures, but they are not equivalent failures.

What I am arguing is narrower: that the political energy being organized around defending DEI, as necessary as that defense is, will not by itself advance decolonial creative practice. It will, at best, restore the conditions that existed before the rollbacks, conditions that were already inadequate for the work.

The more generative question, for those of us who carry this work, is what creative resistance looks like when we stop organizing it primarily as a response to what institutions do. What does it look like when the frame is resurgence rather than resistance? When the primary referent is the continuance of Indigenous knowledge practices, not the opposition to colonial structures?

Leanne Betasamosake Simpson is precise about this distinction. Resurgence, in her framing, is not a political strategy. It is a way of living. Dancing, storytelling, land-based learning, ceremony: these are not tools deployed in response to colonialism. They are the substance of Indigenous life, which colonialism has attempted to interrupt. The interruption is real, but it does not define what the practice is for.

In my own teaching, the moments that mattered most were not the moments of institutional permission or institutional pushback. They were the moments when learners, Indigenous learners in particular, produced work that exceeded what I had designed space for.

Students who submitted presentations in their Indigenous language, requiring listeners to make an effort to translate. Students who submitted one-act plays about community relationships to land and water. Students who made visual art pieces, music, and dances that did not follow the assignment rubric did not do so because they were non-compliant but because the rubric had not been built with enough imagination to anticipate what they had to say.

I did not design those outcomes. What I designed was an assessment architecture that did not pre-close them. I stopped requiring knowledge to be submitted in forms legible only to Western academic convention. I made space, relational and ethical space, not just procedural space, for other modes of knowing to enter. What entered was not what I expected, which is the point.

The creative work of decolonization at the pedagogical level is partly this: refusing to be the sole arbiter of what valid knowledge production looks like. This refusal is not available to an institution in the same way it is available to a teacher, because institutions run on legibility. They require assessment. They require documentation. They require that what happens inside them can be accounted for in terms the institution understands.

This is not a criticism of institutions as such. It is a description of their structural condition. And it is why creative resistance, in its most substantive form, will always exceed what institutional DEI frameworks can protect or what institutional DEI rollbacks can destroy.

There is one more thing to name, and it is the harder one.

My own complicity.

For years before I developed the theoretical and ethical clarity I am writing from now, I taught in ways that reproduced the colonial structures I was embedded in. I taught the curriculum as written. I kept my Indigenous identity in a separate compartment, activated when asked about my Pasifika heritage, set aside when the lesson plan demanded positivistic science methodology. I experienced what Du Bois named double consciousness, not as a theoretical concept but as a daily performance of professional survival.

The development of a decolonial practice was not a switch I flipped. It was a longitudinal process of memory work, relationship building with knowledge keepers, and honest accounting of where I had been complicit. Autoethnography, as methodology, forced me to look at that complicity directly.

I say this because the current conversation about creative resistance, at its most celebratory, tends to position the practitioner as already arrived: the artist, the scholar, the educator who has found their practice of resistance and is now doing the work. What gets lost is the processual nature of that arrival, the years of working inside structures you are also trying to disrupt, the accumulation of small capitulations alongside the accumulation of small refusals, and the ongoing work of not letting the settler institution’s need for your legibility become your own need.

Creative resistance, when it is honest, includes this. It is not only the hula lesson on states of matter. It is also the years before that lesson when I followed the curriculum guide without questioning its epistemological foundations. It is the question of how a practitioner builds the capacity for genuine disruption from inside an institution that trained them to be legible.

The DEI debate, at its center, is a debate about institutional access and institutional resources. These matter. But creative practice, at its center, is a question of what we are accountable to, what relationships we are honouring, and what ways of knowing we are carrying forward across conditions that have never been reliably hospitable.

The institution can grant access or withdraw it. It cannot grant or withdraw the obligation.

That distinction is where the work lives.
