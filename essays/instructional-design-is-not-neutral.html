Instructional Design Is Not Neutral

April 15, 2026

What Indigenous Midwifery Taught Me About Learning

I have come to believe that instructional design is never just instructional design.

People often speak about it as if it were a neutral craft: identify the outcomes, arrange the content, build the assessment, and deliver the course. Clean. Efficient. Professional. But in my work with Indigenous midwifery, I can no longer accept that framing. A curriculum is never neutral. It carries a worldview. It decides, often quietly, what counts as knowledge, whose knowledge matters, and what kind of person a learner is being asked to become.

In my role as a developer and administrator, I have had to realize that ontology must come before epistemology. Before asking what a student knows, I have to ask: what has the system allowed to survive in them? What has been interrupted? As an administrator, I see how easily institutional language and standardized outcomes can bury Indigenous life. My task isn’t just to “build a course”; it is to ensure that the design does not demand that a student leave their personhood at the door.

This is especially visible in the context of midwifery.

Within this field, practitioners have taught me that birth is never “only” clinical. It is relational, spiritual, and historical; deeply tied to land and community continuity. When I approach the administration of these programs, I realized that a curriculum ignoring these dimensions isn’t just incomplete; it’s an instrument of erasure. Therefore, the design process cannot start with procedures. It must start with what must be protected.

That is the shift I keep returning to: the lesson plan is not a verdict. It is an invitation.

Too often, educational frameworks decide who the learner is before they have had the chance to speak. They sort, measure, and label according to systems that claim objectivity but are built on inherited assumptions. I am less interested in that kind of design. I am interested in building learning environments, from the technical back-end to the classroom culture, that leave room for self-location, ancestry, and the “unreachable remainder” of knowledge that cannot be reduced to a rubric.

That remainder is where the real learning happens.

Indigenous learners do not arrive as empty vessels for us to fill with “competencies.” They arrive carrying land, story, protocol, and responsibility. If the administrative structure of a program cannot make room for that, then we are asking them to split themselves in half to participate. That is not education. That is extraction.

My own approach has been shaped by years of working at the intersection of Western science and Indigenous reciprocity; from Ontario to the Cordillera region of Luzon. I have seen how easily institutions mistake structure for wisdom. I have also seen how transformative learning becomes when the environment is rooted in relationship rather than control.

This is why the work at an Indigenous-focused institution is so vital to me. It is a place where educational sovereignty is a living practice. The goal of our midwifery modules isn’t just to produce graduates who meet clinical requirements; it is to build a structure that supports people who carry knowledge with humility and cultural authority.

When I imagine the architecture of a midwifery module, I don’t want it to begin with a textbook definition. I want the design to facilitate a return to homeplace.

The design should invite students to reflect on the birthing stories carried in their own communities and what their bodies remember. Only after the structure has honored that ground should we introduce clinical standards. And even then, as a developer, I must frame those standards carefully: not as the ultimate truth, but as one set of tools within a larger field of care.

Western clinical standards save lives. They have a place. But as an administrator, I believe they should not stand as the highest authority over everything else. The challenge in design is to place clinical knowledge inside a wider ethics of care—one where ceremony and community sovereignty are essential, not decorative.

This is where design becomes more than design.

A course can either flatten the learner or support them in becoming more fully themselves. A digital environment: the LMS, the visuals, the ethics of AI. They can either reproduce the sterile logic of an institution or become a space that feels alive and resonant.

I do not want to build a curriculum that simply teaches students how to function inside a system that was never made for them. I want to help build a structure that allows them to move through that system without surrendering their ontological ground.

That is the heart of my philosophy.

Instructional design can do more than organize information. It can protect what is fragile and support the return of knowledge to the people who have always carried it. In the context of Indigenous midwifery, this is urgent. We aren’t just designing for a credential; we are designing so that Indigenous ways of being can remain whole.
