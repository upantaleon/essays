When the Lab Becomes a Language: What Chemistry Education Is Missing

March 20, 2026

A science classroom is not a neutral space. Neither is the way we study it.

Imagine a first-year university chemistry student standing at a lab bench. The hot plate is not working. The data does not match what it should. A classmate three benches over seems to be finishing up with no trouble. The TA is busy. The report is due in forty minutes.

What happens next is one of the least studied questions in science education research. Not what the student does procedurally, but what they experience. What do they feel? What does their body do? What does the institution, in that moment, communicate to them about whether they belong here?

These are not soft questions. They sit at the heart of who stays in science and who leaves.

The Problem with How We Study Science Learning

Science education research has a methods problem. It is not a crisis, but it is a persistent narrowing of what the field allows itself to see.

The dominant approach to studying student experience in science classrooms is quantitative. Surveys. Likert scales. Pre- and post-test scores. Engagement metrics. These tools are useful for some purposes. They can tell you, in aggregate, that students who report higher science identity scores persist at higher rates. They can tell you that anxiety correlates with lower lab performance. They produce clean data and publishable results.

What they cannot tell you is what it is actually like to be a student in a chemistry lab at the moment something goes wrong.

This is not a minor gap. It is a structural limitation. Quantitative methods flatten experience into numbers, and in doing so, they erase precisely the dimensions of student life that matter most for understanding equity in science education: the embodied, the affective, the relational, the cultural. The feelings. The mood. The way the body tenses when a TA walks past without stopping. The specific weight of not knowing whether you are failing because you made a mistake or because you were never supposed to be here.

A second problem compounds the first. Most research on affect in science education, the growing body of work that does try to study emotions, identity, and engagement, frames affect as a barrier. Anxiety is a barrier to learning chemistry content. Low science identity is a barrier to persistence. The question the research asks is: how do we remove the barrier so the real learning can happen?

This framing is worth examining. It treats affect as an obstacle to be managed rather than as something constitutive of the learning experience itself. It positions the emotional and relational dimensions of science education as noise to be filtered out rather than signal to be interpreted. And it keeps the research firmly inside a curriculum- and instructor-centred logic: the goal is student performance, and affect is relevant only insofar as it gets in the way.

What this framing cannot accommodate is a more fundamental question: what if the affect is the curriculum? What if what students learn about themselves as knowers, as scientists, as people who do or do not belong in this space, is inseparable from the procedural content of the lab?

Heteroglossia: A Different Way of Reading the Lab

To understand what science education research is missing, it helps to borrow a concept from an unlikely source: the Russian literary theorist Mikhail Bakhtin.

Bakhtin developed the concept of heteroglossia to describe the condition of language in the novel. Any text, he argued, contains multiple voices, multiple registers, multiple social and ideological positions speaking simultaneously and often in tension with each other. A character’s speech carries not just their individual meaning but the accumulated weight of every social context in which that kind of language has been used before.

The concept travels further than you might expect. A chemistry laboratory is, in Bakhtin’s sense, a heteroglossic space. It contains multiple voices speaking at once. There is the authoritative voice of the lab manual, with its precise procedural instructions and its implicit message about what counts as correct. There is the voice of the TA, who carries the culture of the research university and communicates, often without intending to, who belongs in this space and who is struggling. There is the voice of the student’s prior schooling, which has already told them something about whether science is for people like them. And there is the student’s own voice, formed through culture, language, family, faith, and prior experience, which may or may not find a place in the dominant discourse of the laboratory.

These voices are not equal. The lab manual does not negotiate with the student. The TA’s tacit knowledge about how a real scientist behaves does not announce itself as a cultural assumption; it presents as professional competence. The institutional voice of the laboratory is experienced by students as natural, neutral, and authoritative. Their own voices, when they conflict with it, tend to register as deficiency.

This is not a metaphor. It is a description of a power structure that operates every time a student enters a lab.

Not All Failure Is Created Equal

The concept of stigmatized failure brings this into sharper focus.

Failure is treated in most educational discourse as a neutral event, a correctable technical outcome. You made an error; the error is identified; you learn from it and proceed. Progressive pedagogies have worked hard to rehabilitate failure, to position it as a productive part of the learning process, to tell students that it is acceptable to fail.

The problem, as educational researcher Feigenbaum (2021) documents, is that institutions tell students it is acceptable to fail while simultaneously treating failure as a summative judgment with real consequences. The lab report is graded. The cumulative average matters for program admission. The TA notices who is struggling. The student knows their peers are watching.

Failure is not a neutral correctable event. It is a socially situated experience whose meaning and weight depend entirely on who is failing, in front of whom, in what institutional context, and with what prior history.

Think about two students sitting that same first-year chemistry lab.

The first grew up watching family members work in science. She attended a well-resourced secondary school with strong lab facilities. She looks like the people whose photographs hang in the department hallway. When her experiment fails, she experiences it as a correctable technical setback. The institution has already told her, in a hundred ways, that she belongs here. A mistake does not threaten that.

The second is the first in his family to attend university. He comes from a community that has historically been excluded from scientific institutions. He has been told, explicitly or implicitly, that science is not for people like him. When the same experiment fails, he experiences it in a categorically different register. It confirms something the institution has already been whispering.

The failure is identical. The experience of failure is not.

This is what quantitative affect research cannot see. A survey that asks students to rate their anxiety on a five-point scale before and after a lab session produces data. It does not produce an account of what anxiety feels like when it is layered over a history of exclusion. It does not tell you whose anxiety is a temporary emotional state and whose is a rational response to a genuinely hostile institutional environment.

The Body Knows What the Survey Does Not Ask

Phenomenological research offers a different kind of evidence. This is the qualitative methodology that focuses on describing lived experience from the inside, in its full texture, rather than reducing it to categories and scores.

When students are asked not “how anxious were you” but “what was it like,” the accounts that emerge are thick with detail that surveys erase. The body tenses. The hands move too quickly or freeze entirely. The mind tracks the TA’s position in the room. The student calculates whether asking for help will mark them as incompetent or save them from a worse outcome. Time accelerates. The specific sensation of watching a classmate finish while you are still at the beginning of the procedure is not anxiety in the abstract. It is a precise, embodied, socially comparative experience that carries the full weight of the student’s history with institutional science.

This kind of evidence does not produce clean statistics. It produces something more useful: an account of the laboratory as students actually experience it, which turns out to be a far more complex, socially charged, and emotionally layered environment than the curriculum documents suggest.

The lab, seen this way, is not just a place where students learn titration or spectroscopy. It is a place where they learn something about themselves. About whether they are the kind of person who belongs in science. About whether failure here means temporary setback or confirmed inadequacy. About whether their ways of knowing have a place in this institutional discourse or need to be left at the door.

What the Field Is Missing

Chemistry education research has made genuine progress. There is more attention to student identity, more use of qualitative methods, and more interest in the affective dimensions of laboratory learning than there was a decade ago.

But the structural limitations remain. The field still predominantly asks: how do we help students succeed within the existing laboratory structure? It asks this question with increasingly sophisticated tools. It does not ask often enough: what does the existing laboratory structure teach students about who counts as a scientist, and is that a lesson we should be delivering?

The laboratory, examined carefully, is a site where multiple inequalities converge and are reproduced. Prior access to scientific culture, linguistic and cultural capital, the racial and gender demographics of the discipline, the implicit standards of what competent scientific behaviour looks like. All of these operate in the lab, shaping who experiences failure as correctable and who experiences it as confirmation.

Research that flattens this complexity into survey scores does not just fail to capture it. It actively obscures it, producing accounts of student experience that look rigorous but leave the structural conditions of inequality entirely unexamined.

What the field needs is not just better methods, though better methods would help. It needs a different set of questions. Questions that start not with “how do students perform” but with “what is it like to be a student here.” Questions that treat the laboratory not as a neutral instructional environment but as a culturally and historically situated space that carries, in every piece of equipment and every assessment rubric, a set of assumptions about whose knowledge counts.

The student at the lab bench with the broken hot plate, forty minutes before the report is due, is not just experiencing a technical problem. She is experiencing the full weight of an institution’s assumptions about competence, belonging, and failure. Whether those assumptions include her is not a question her anxiety score can answer.

That experience deserves research methods adequate to its complexity. And it deserves educators willing to ask what the lab is actually teaching.
