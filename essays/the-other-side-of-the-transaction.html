The Other Side of the Transaction: When the Institution Sees Your Body But Not Your Knowledge

May 19, 2026

Why the "Self-Indigenization" critique in academia is only telling half the story.

Estella Carpi recently named something that many of us have been feeling for a long time: the “self-indigenization syndrome.”

Carpi, Estella. 2026. “Where Does the ‘Self-Indigenization’ Syndrome Come From? Critical Reflections on ‘Research Legitimacy’ in Contemporary Academia.” Member Voices, Fieldsights, April 30. https://www.culanth.org/fieldsights/series/where-does-the-self-indigenization-syndrome-come-from-critical-reflections-on-research-legitimacy-and-the-tokenism-of-contemporary-academia

Carpi (2026) describes the white Global North researcher who performs proximity to the South as a strategy for moral legitimacy. It is an incisive, necessary critique of how decolonial language is absorbed into institutional compliance, stripped of its political teeth, and redeployed as a career management tool.

I agree with her diagnosis. But I have been thinking about what her framework leaves out.

Carpi’s scholar is the outsider (white, positioned in a UK institution) anxious about their lack of belonging. Her advice to them is inward-facing: process your positionality, stop manufacturing proximity you don’t have.

This is sound advice. But it does not apply to me.

I am a mixed-heritage Pasifika scholar who has spent many years in science education across Ontario, Australia, and the Philippines. I am not manufacturing proximity to the South. I am the South, institutionally relocated. And from where I stand, the injury is not about moral anxiety. It is about erasure.

The Condition of Racialized Diglossia

What Carpi cannot see from her vantage point is that the Northern academy doesn’t just ignore racialized scholars, and it absorbs them. It instrumentalizes our biographies as diversity tokens, while continuing to evaluate our actual knowledge through Anglophone standards that were designed to exclude us in the first place.

I call this racialized diglossia.

Borrowing from Bakhtin, I use this term to describe the structural demand that a racialized scholar operate in two registers simultaneously: the relational, vernacular knowledge that constitutes our actual epistemic formation, and the standardized “academic code” that the institution recognizes as legitimate.

Every seminar, peer review, and grant application requires a translation that is never neutral. It systematically privileges the target language and discounts what was lost in transit.

The Problem of Epistemic Inclusion

In my own work, I have used the Medicine Wheel as an autoethnographic research framework. It’s not a cultural gesture; however, it’s an epistemological claim about what constitutes rigorous, complete knowledge.

But in the Canadian educational landscape, it is routinely rendered illegible. It’s not that the argument is weighed and found wanting; it’s that the institutional infrastructure doesn’t even have the criteria to weigh it.

The system is happy to admit my body as a way to diversify the seminar room. It is far less willing to accept my epistemology as a way to change what knowledge that room can produce.

Integration vs. Inclusion

The most painful example of this, as Dr. George Nana Dei has long argued, is the difference between integration and inclusion.

The academy has, in many formal respects, achieved integration. We are on the panels, the grant lists, and the faculty rosters. But it has not achieved inclusion. The radical reorganization of an institution’s evaluative premises around frameworks that it did not produce.

As long as decolonial critique remains focused only on the moral anxiety of the white researcher, it remains a theory written from only one side of the room. It addresses the person managing their anxiety, but it misses the person who is managing their erasure.
