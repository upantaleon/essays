The PISA Paradox

April 23, 2026

An inquiry into the "statistical erasure" of Indigenous knowledge and the limitations of the OECD’s equity framework.

The Prestige of the Metric

Every few years, the world of education stops to check its pulse against the PISA results. The OECD’s Programme for International Student Assessment is the gold standard of global education metrics, a massive data-gathering exercise that tells us who is “winning” at math, science, and reading.

In the 2022 Volume I results, titled The State of Learning and Equity in Education, there is much to celebrate on the surface. We see a rigorous attempt to define “equity” not just as equal scores but as a system where a student’s socio-economic background does not predetermine their destiny. But as a sociologist of education, I find that what is left out of the report is often as significant as what is included.

The Mirage of Equity

The PISA report defines equity through two lenses: fairness and inclusion. Fairness means socio-economic status (SES) shouldn’t predict performance; inclusion means everyone hits a baseline.

From a social justice perspective, this is “Equity Lite.” It treats equity as a statistical correlation to be smoothed out rather than a historical and structural condition to be dismantled. By focusing on making every student proficient in a Western, standardized curriculum, we risk rewarding what Pierre Bourdieu called “Cultural Capital”—the hidden codes of the dominant class—while dismissing the diverse epistemologies of those on the margins.

The “Statistical Erasure” of Indigenous Education

Perhaps the most striking gap in the global discourse is the invisibility of Indigenous students. While the report disaggregates data for “immigrant background,” Indigenous status is largely subsumed under general socio-economic disadvantage.

This is more than a clerical oversight; it is a form of statistical erasure. When we measure Indigenous students against a global metric of “literacy” without accounting for language revitalization, land-based learning, or colonial history, we apply a deficit model. We see what is “missing” according to the OECD, rather than what is present in terms of cultural sovereignty and unique knowledge systems.

The Resilience Trap

One of the report’s favourite categories is the “resilient student”—the individual who performs in the top quarter despite coming from a disadvantaged background.

On the surface, this is an inspiring narrative. However, a critical sociological view reveals a “Resilience Trap.” By celebrating the few who “make it,” the focus shifts from the state’s failure to provide adequate resources to the individual’s ability to overcome systemic neglect. It risks validating a colonial education system that requires Indigenous and marginalized youth to survive the school, rather than being nourished by it.

Beyond the Scoreboard

If we are to move toward true social justice education, we must look beyond the scoreboard. PISA offers a powerful diagnostic for socio-economic disparity, but it is not a roadmap for decolonization.

True equity requires more than just moving the needle on a math test; it requires recognizing that there are multiple ways of knowing and many definitions of a “better life” that a standardized test can never capture. As we digest the 2022 results, we must ask: Are we building systems that empower all students, or are we simply perfecting the art of assimilation?
