The Inversion of Identity

May 12, 2026

Postmodernism and the Aesthetics of Existence

People often say postmodernism is what gave rise to identity politics. It sounds neat, even convincing at first. But the more you sit with it, the less it holds together. That claim tends to blur very different intellectual traditions into one story that’s a little too convenient.

Identity politics, at least in many of its forms, leans on the idea that there’s something stable underneath—some core that can be named, defended, and organized around. Postmodernism moves in almost the opposite direction. It doesn’t try to ground identity; it keeps poking at it, asking where it came from, who benefits from it, and why we trust it so much in the first place.

Foucault is usually dragged into this conversation, often in ways that miss what he was actually doing. He wasn’t trying to tell us what madness or sexuality really is. He was more interested in how we came to talk about them the way we do. That is, how institutions, practices, and forms of knowledge quietly shape what counts as “normal” or even “real.” In Discipline and Punish, for example, the point isn’t just about prisons. It’s about how systems produce certain kinds of people that are legible, classifiable, and manageable.

Once you see that, identity starts to look less like a truth and more like something that’s been organized, maybe even imposed. Foucault was wary of turning identity into something you have to be faithful to, like a rulebook. There’s a risk there: what starts as liberation can circle back into a new kind of constraint.

That suspicion ties into a broader resistance to essentialism and the idea that people or things have some fixed essence underneath everything. Postmodern thinkers tend to treat that idea with a lot of skepticism. Derrida, for instance, shows how meaning never quite settles. Words don’t carry neat, self-contained definitions; they lean on other words, other contrasts. Meaning shifts and slips; however, it never fully lands.

If that’s the case, then identity categories aren’t as solid as they sometimes appear. They’re assembled, negotiated, and always a bit unstable. Useful, but not foundational in any final sense.

Judith Butler takes this into the terrain of gender and social life. Her argument that identity is something performed, repeated, and stabilized over time lands differently once you think of it in everyday terms. It’s not that identity is fake; it’s that it’s maintained. And when people start treating those patterns as fixed truths, they harden into expectations. You get norms, then pressure, then quiet forms of policing, sometimes from outside, sometimes from within.

Butler isn’t calling for identities to disappear. That’s not really the point. It’s more about keeping them open, a little unsettled, so they don’t close in on the people who inhabit them.

Nietzsche adds another layer, and it’s a sharper one. His idea of ressentiment and this reactive way of forming identity against something else still sting when you apply it today. If who you are is built mainly in opposition, then you’re still tied to what you’re resisting. That dependence doesn’t just go away because the intention is political or moral. Sometimes it quietly reproduces the same structure, just with the signs flipped.

So across all these thinkers, there’s a shared unease with anything that claims to be fixed, final, or beyond question. Identity, in that light, starts to look more like a tool and something you use when it helps, something you rethink when it doesn’t.

That’s why the equation of postmodernism with rigid identity politics feels off. Not just slightly inaccurate, but inverted. One depends on stabilizing the subject; the other keeps destabilizing it.

A better way to think about the postmodern impulse might be this: it’s less about declaring what you are and more about staying in the process of becoming. There’s a creative element to it. This was what Foucault, at one point, called an “aesthetics of existence.” Life isn’t something you simply express; it’s something you shape, revise, and experiment with.

That doesn’t mean abandoning identity altogether. Labels still do work in the world. But they don’t get the final word. They stay provisional, secondary to the ongoing task of figuring out how to live and who you might still become.
