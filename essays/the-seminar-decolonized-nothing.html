The Seminar Decolonized Nothing

March 30, 2026

and That’s the Point

There’s a peculiar discomfort that comes from sitting in a room where people talk about decolonization while everything about the room stays the same. I’ve been in that room many times. Most recently, I sat in it through a Zoom screen, watching graduate students at one of Canada’s education faculties present on settler colonialism, racial knowledge production, necropolitics, and the postcolonial history of Kenyan education. The irony didn’t escape me. It was the whole point.

The word decolonization gets used a lot these days. It shows up in universities, corporate statements, and activist meetings. But its meaning changes depending on where you’re standing when you say it. I come to it with some history. I once taught science in the Cordillera region of Luzon, in Benguet province in the Philippines, before completing a doctorate. Moving from an Indigenous mountain community in the postcolonial Philippines to a graduate seminar in settler colonial Ontario gave me a lasting sense of what decolonization can mean … and what it can’t.

Canada is not the Philippines, and Ontario is not Benguet. That distinction matters more than people tend to admit. The Philippines achieved formal independence in 1946. The colonizers left. What remained was what Aimé Césaire called the mark with the architecture of colonialism in knowledge, language, and institutions that outlives the empire itself.

In Canada, the colonizers never left. Colonial power still lives here through the legislature, the courts, the universities, and the school system. Indigenous nations never achieved flag independence because settlers stayed. Patrick Wolfe captured this plainly: settler colonialism isn’t an event that ends; it’s a structure that adapts. Confusing this with postcolonialism, as some anti-racist education frameworks do, leads to real political and analytical confusion.

I thought about that while a student presented on Kenya.

Kenya’s story after independence is one that Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o has been telling for decades. The country gained independence from Britain in 1963. The political handover was real, but the education system hardly changed. The colonial school structure remained almost the same, along with the curriculum, the examinations, and the language of instruction. English stayed at the top. Swahili was named the national language, but in practice, it remained secondary. That gap between declaration and practice wasn’t a small inconsistency; it defined the system itself.

Ngũgĩ argued in Decolonising the Mind that this wasn’t accidental. Language isn’t only a means of communication; it shapes how a child learns to see the world. When the language of school is the colonizer’s language, the child learns more than grammar. She learns that her own language, and her community’s ways of knowing, belong to a lower order. Ngũgĩ wrote Decolonising the Mind in English, then stopped writing in English altogether. That decision wasn’t symbolic. It was the argument lived out.

Fanon had already seen this dynamic. In The Wretched of the Earth, he warned that the educated national class, trained inside colonial institutions, would inherit and preserve them after independence. Not necessarily out of corruption, but because the colonial school shapes how they understand knowledge and transformation itself. Kenya’s curriculum kept following British epistemological frameworks for years. African history entered slowly. Indigenous Kenyan knowledge system and its agricultural, ecological, and social wisdom built across generations where structured to remained outside what the system recognized as knowledge at all.

You can see the same pattern elsewhere. Julius Nyerere recognized it in Tanzania. In 1967, he wrote Education for Self-Reliance, calling for schools grounded in local realities rather than colonial objectives. Tanzania tried to rebuild education from the ground up, but the pressures of global economics eventually pulled it back toward older models. Still, Nyerere named the problem clearly: independence could not be real if education kept reproducing colonial purposes.

That brings me to James Baldwin. He said the camera is never neutral. Long before the photo is taken, the person holding the camera has chosen what to focus on and what to leave out. The image doesn’t show you the world; it shows you a world, framed by someone’s perspective.

The graduate seminar is a camera. The anti-racist framework within it is a camera too. Both point sincerely toward injustice, but both also make choices: whose theories count, what language we use, what credentials make a voice credible. Those choices are built into the institution itself. The same institution the seminar was trying to critique.

I don’t think the seminar was meaningless. I think it was a perfect reflection of the problem it wanted to analyze. That’s not a failure. It’s a condition. The students presented their ideas inside the same language, citation systems, and digital platforms of the very order they were critiquing. I was watching them through an American tech company’s interface. None of us were outside the problem. There is no outside. The real question is whether we can be honest about where we stand when we speak.

The students who moved me the most were the ones who recognized that. They didn’t try to escape the contradiction. They spoke from inside it. That, as George Sefa Dei reminds us, is what true positionality means. It isn’t a short paragraph about who you are. It’s an epistemological commitment; that is to say where you are standing before you say what you see.

The seminar didn’t decolonize anything. Neither did Ngũgĩ’s book, in the end, decolonize Kenya. Neither has any curriculum reform in Canada decolonized the land or the institutions that sit on it. That doesn’t mean we should give up. It means we should be precise. Decolonization is not something that can be completed inside existing institutions. It’s a practice that continues in tension with them, recognizing that the same institutions also shape who we are.

The most honest thing the seminar did was reveal that truth.

The camera is never neutral. But maybe the work begins when we learn to say that, even while we are the ones holding it.
