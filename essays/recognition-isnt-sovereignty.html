Recognition Isn't Sovereignty

July 8, 2026

A Five-Year Check-In on Indigenous Research Governance

In 2020, I argued that Indigenous research sovereignty, meaning actual Indigenous governance of research involving Indigenous peoples, was achievable within Canada’s federal funding system. Five years later, I went back to check. The answer isn’t encouraging.

Here’s the distinction that matters, borrowed from Glen Coulthard’s critique of recognition politics: when a state “recognizes” Indigenous knowledge or rights, it usually does so on terms the state itself sets. The recognized party has to become legible to the institution doing the recognizing. That’s not sovereignty; it’s managed inclusion, and it’s easy to mistake for progress because it produces real, visible activity: new grants, new advisory bodies, and revised guidelines.

What it doesn’t produce is a shift in who decides.

Since 2020, Canada’s Tri-Council agencies have expanded Indigenous-specific funding streams, added Indigenous reviewers to committees, and piloted procedural accommodations. All of that is real. None of it has moved adjudicative authority off federal agencies and onto Indigenous governing institutions. Funding decisions, including decisions about who counts as Indigenous for eligibility purposes, are still made by the same bodies they were made by five years ago.

I found the same pattern showing up outside research funding entirely, in places you wouldn’t necessarily think to compare. Wherever a settler institution incorporates Indigenous knowledge at the level of content while keeping the authority to define what counts as valid knowledge, you get the same structure with a different name. Adding Indigenous material to a framework doesn’t redistribute who governs the framework.

Comparative cases outside Canada made this harder to excuse. Other jurisdictions have moved further, some toward binding enforcement, some toward actual transferred jurisdiction over data and research governance. Canada’s model is advisory participation plus institutional discretion, which is the weakest combination available among the countries I looked at.

The uncomfortable conclusion: five years of programmatic activity within an unchanged governance structure isn’t decolonization. It’s the management of the status quo, and it’s easy to confuse the two if you’re only counting new initiatives rather than asking where decision-making authority actually sits.
