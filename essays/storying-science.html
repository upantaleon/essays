Storying Science

May 1, 2026

Decolonizing the Curriculum Through Relational Knowledge

In many schools, the push to “include” Indigenous science ends up looking like a patchwork of cultural facts dropped into a Western framework. Lessons on medicinal plants may appear beside the periodic table, or oral histories might be added as “context” to a study of ecosystems. While well-intentioned, this additive model fails to capture the living depth of Indigenous knowledge systems. My recent research suggests that meaningful integration requires something far more transformative. Our concept of knowledge may shift from a linear curriculum of facts to a recursive, spiritual, and ethical practice rooted in relational understanding.

Indigenous science is not a parallel track to Western science; it’s a complex ecology of knowledge that operates through story, relationship, and responsibility. Frameworks like the Medicine Wheel or Four Directions remind us that learning is cyclical, not hierarchical. These frameworks encode balance between the physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual aspects of discovery. When schools use such models as organizing principles (not as decoration), they stop treating Indigenous knowledge as a guest in the classroom and begin to relearn what learning itself means. Science, in this sense, becomes an act of positioning: students locate themselves and their communities inside the knowledge they build, rather than imitating experiments designed for someone else’s world.

Storying as Science Communication

At the heart of this process is storying. This is a method and methodology of co-creation where words are not vessels for static meaning but living participants in dialogue. In storying, Indigenous languages don’t exist to be translated or simplified but to provoke reflection and ethical relation. The story becomes a teaching spirit in itself, carrying scientific, moral, and metaphysical layers all at once. This approach challenges academia’s obsession with “clarity” as the removal of complexity. True clarity, from an Indigenous lens, comes from seeing many relationships at once.

When science communication adopts storying, it stops being a monologue of findings and becomes a conversation with place, spirit, and community. Knowledge then transforms from property into relationship. Perhaps something that grows through care, accountability, and reciprocity.

The Liminal Zone of Knowledge

Western science still has gifts to offer; however, only when it sees itself as one voice among many. The intersection between Western and Indigenous sciences is a liminal zone, a space of encounter where both sides can evolve through humility. Current pedagogical models like inquiry-based learning or technology integration can flourish here, but only if they release their colonial habit of using Western validation as the ultimate measure of truth.

This means redefining innovation itself. Rather than chasing the next gadget or metric, innovation can be reimagined as a spiritual expression of ancestral gifts, as something that emerges through reverence, not extraction. Protecting Indigenous knowledge, then, is not about locking it away but honouring the relational protocols that govern its sharing. Ethics, credibility, and creativity are all tied to how one visits, listens, and gives back.

The Researcher as Relational Being

Decolonization, in the end, is not an academic technique but an identity shift. The researcher must move from extracting to embodying, from interpreting to participating. My work suggests that the educator’s journey toward decolonization mirrors that of the storyteller, who speaks not to inform but to invite relationship. Using autoethnography, that is, writing from and through our lived experience, becomes a way to trace our complicity and transformation.

This approach demands that research itself become an offering. It asks, "Who benefits from this knowledge? Who is healed by it?” Only when the answers include the land, the ancestors, and the community can scholarship transcend performance and become something sacred.

In this way, integrating Indigenous science into classrooms is not a curricular reform. Let’s consider that it’s a spiritual and ethical reorientation of education itself. Learning stops being about mastery and starts being about relationships. Science becomes ceremony. And teaching, when done with relational love, becomes a way of giving thanks to the world that sustains us.
