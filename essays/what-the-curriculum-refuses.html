What the Curriculum Refuses

June 17, 2026

On colonial science education, the politics of refusal, and what it costs to carry Indigenous knowledge into settler institutions

My grandparents worked on plantations near Hilo. To leave the sleeping cabins at night, they showed identity tags. At market, finding somewhere to eat a meal required knowing which places would seat them. These were not exceptional incidents. They were the structure of daily life under labor conditions designed to extract work while denying personhood. My grandparents did not theorize this. They survived it, and then they told the stories, and the stories became my first education in what it costs to inhabit a body the dominant order has decided not to fully recognize.

I am a science educator. I am also, in every institution I have worked in, an outsider within: present in the building, tolerated in the curriculum meeting, and absent from the epistemological foundation of what the curriculum actually teaches.

The science curriculum in Ontario does not include Indigenous knowledge. It includes references to Indigenous knowledge. The difference is structural, not cosmetic. When a curriculum document says that students will learn to respect diverse ways of knowing while organizing every learning expectation around observation, hypothesis, and empirical verification as universal foundations of inquiry, it has already made a decision. The architecture of the curriculum treats Western scientific method as the measure against which all other ways of knowing are evaluated. Indigenous knowledge enters that architecture as a supplement, as cultural enrichment, as context. It does not enter as an alternative epistemological foundation, because the architecture was not built to accommodate one.

This is not a recent oversight. It is the operating logic of colonial science education, and it persists through every curriculum revision, every professional development workshop on Indigenous content, every reconciliation initiative that claims to integrate Indigenous perspectives while preserving the hierarchy in which those perspectives are judged against Western scientific standards.

I spent over two decades accumulating evidence of this. Lesson plans, journals, photographs of student work, margin notes on curriculum documents. One margin note I kept returning to said only, “What about their attitudes?” I wrote it after watching teacher candidates complete a science lab. They were technically competent. They followed the procedure, recorded the data, and wrote the conclusions. What they were not doing was thinking. There was no curiosity about what the experiment was actually claiming about the natural world, no question about whether the protocol might be missing something, no engagement with the knowledge they were supposedly constructing. They were performing science. The curriculum asked them to, and they did, and that was enough.

What the curriculum was not asking them to do was consider what science, as a set of institutional practices, excludes. It was not asking them to notice that the natural world has been in a relationship with human communities for thousands of years before the arrival of laboratory protocols and that those communities produced sophisticated, tested, relational knowledge of that world. It was not asking them to think about what it means to teach scientific observation as though the observer has no obligations to what is being observed.

I developed a habit of supplementing this. Teaching states of matter, I used hula to embody how atoms and compounds move through phase changes. The movement was not decorative. Hula is a form of knowledge transmission that encodes relationships between the body, the natural world, and ancestral understanding. Teaching it alongside the curriculum was not an enhancement. It was a collision between a curriculum that treats the body as a neutral instrument for following experimental procedures and a tradition that treats the body as the primary site where knowledge between humans and the natural world is made and held.

That collision is productive, but it is not comfortable, and the discomfort is the point. The curriculum can accommodate the hula lesson. It can absorb it as a creative teaching strategy, a culturally responsive practice, and a way of engaging diverse learners. What it cannot do is recognize the hula lesson as a methodological challenge to its own epistemological foundation, because doing that would require acknowledging that the foundation is not universal. It would require the curriculum to take seriously the possibility that it is one way of knowing the natural world, not the way.

At some point in the research underlying this piece, I made a decision that changed how I understood the ethics of academic work. I had been conducting a longitudinal autoethnographic study, coding my own professional experiences and analyzing the structural features of colonial science education. The natural move, in a conventional qualitative study, would have been to code the knowledge shared with me by Elders and Knowledge Keepers as data: record the conversations, assign labels, extract insights, and publish findings.

I did not do that.

The knowledge that Elders shared with me in conversation was not data. It was a gift, made in a specific relational context, with obligations attached that did not include publication in a Western academic journal. Transforming that knowledge into coded, extractable data would have reproduced exactly the logic I was trying to analyze: the logic that treats all knowledge as available for academic collection, regardless of the relationships in which that knowledge was generated and the protocols that govern its transmission.

I call this a politics of refusal. It is not a retreat from research. It is a methodological position: that there are forms of knowledge a Western academic study cannot ethically include and that naming this boundary clearly and deliberately is part of the research’s contribution. Eve Tuck and K. Wayne Yang have argued that decolonization is not a metaphor, that it is not accomplished by adopting Indigenous language or content while preserving the structural logic of extraction. The same principle applies to research methods. You do not decolonize a study by adding Indigenous knowledge to a Western analytical framework. You decolonize it, if that word applies at all, by refusing the logic that treats all knowledge as extractable.

The day-to-day experience of navigating this in a settler institution has a texture that I can only describe as ecological. Colonialism in education does not arrive as a singular event. It is the curriculum document that acknowledges Indigenous ways of knowing in its preamble and then organizes every learning expectation around a single epistemological tradition. It is the professional development session that teaches teachers to integrate Indigenous content without changing any of the structural conditions under which Indigenous students sit in classrooms. It is the de-streaming of Ontario’s Grade 9 science course, presented as curriculum modernization, with no public evidence of meaningful Indigenous involvement in its design. These are not separate incidents. They are a system, and the system reproduces itself precisely through the gestures that appear to address it.

Teaching under these conditions is an exercise in permanent urgency. You are not working toward a future in which the colonial structures will have been corrected. You are working now, with students who are living the effects of those structures now, in a curriculum that is enforcing those structures now. The question is not whether to work within the system or outside it. You are in it. The question is whether you are honest about what the system is doing.

I finished writing this piece the same way I finish the hula. Not at an ending but at a completion: a return to the same ground, with what the movement produced. My grandparents showed their identity tags and told the stories. I carry those stories into Ontario science classrooms and into academic manuscripts and into the principled refusal to hand Elder knowledge over to a coding system. None of this resolves the colonial structure. It maintains a position within it that the structure cannot fully absorb. That is not nothing. In an ecology of urgency, it may be the most precise thing available.
