What the Institution Cannot Reach

March 31, 2026

the seminar part 2

Last evening, I attended a graduate seminar on social justice education over Zoom, a virtual table of researchers gathered across time zones, each bringing their work into that small digital square. The conversation moved easily through theory and evidence, but beneath it all ran something quieter: the institution’s ability to make its own violence appear as neutral procedure.

There is a moment in teaching that I have lived in classrooms across Ontario, Australia, and the Cordillera region of Luzon, when you realize the curriculum you are delivering has already decided who your students are before they arrive. It has already decided what counts as knowledge, what counts as a body worth knowing, and what counts as a legitimate way of being in the world.

The lesson plan is already a verdict.

It took me thirty years inside that verdict before I could finally name it. My doctoral research gave me the language: ontological–ethical–epistemological. Being, ethics, and knowledge are inseparable, not stacked layers but a single, living condition that colonialism seeks to disrupt at the root.

What follows are reflections drawn from that seminar and from a lifetime inside classrooms, an effort to trace what the institution reaches for and what always escapes its grasp.

The Architecture of Survival

The session opened with a presentation on Black spirituality within a large Catholic school board. The argument was that Black spirituality has never been outside Catholicism and has always existed within it.

I understand the intent. It tries to claim pedagogical legitimacy for Black spiritual heritage inside an institution that has long suppressed it, to say, you belong here.

But belonging is not the same as being recognized on your own terms.

Black spirituality entered Catholic institutional space through the coerced gate of the slave trade and colonial evangelization. What we call its “presence” within Catholicism is, in truth, the survival of African cosmological systems beneath a Catholic veneer. Candomblé, Vodou, Santería each bear witness to this survival. These are African ontologies wearing Catholic clothing in order to live. That clothing was never freely chosen.

For teachers standing before Black students from Caribbean, West African, or Haitian communities, this distinction is not abstract. Those students already know in their bodies that syncretism was a survival strategy. To tell them their heritage has “always belonged” in the Church asks them to misread their own history.

The more generative move is to hold the tension: here is the institution, here is what your heritage carried through it, and here is what it could not reach. That unreachable remainder is where the ontology lives.

The Algorithmic Verdict

Later, another presenter spoke about AI in education. The framework used was a SWOT analysis: Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats.

At first, it sounded critical, but SWOT is a corporate management tool. It assumes the organization itself is the measure of value and asks how adoption can be optimized, not whether the technology should exist at all.

In a social justice context, that is already a concession. It accepts the institution’s frame before the critique begins.

The deeper question, raised by scholars of epistemic injustice, is whether AI tools create conditions where racialized students and teachers are systematically disbelieved when they report harm.

When a Black student points out that an AI-generated description of their community is wrong, and the institution treats the algorithm as more “objective” than the person, that is not a technical glitch. It is a social relation.

Generative AI is built on data that is overwhelmingly Western and white. It reproduces that gravity at scale. For students whose heritage includes Yoruba cosmology, Igorot science, or Cree land-based knowledge, AI reflects their erasure back to them with the calm authority of a system that appears to have no author and therefore no accountability.

Homeplace and the Archaeology of Self

The most resonant moment came from a researcher studying Black Canadian girls and arts education.

She turned to the concept of homeplace, the domestic and communal spaces Black women have long created as zones of dignity and resistance.

Applied to arts pedagogy, creative practice itself becomes a homeplace. It rejects the “therapeutic” frame. A homeplace is not a branded safe space with posters and a land acknowledgment. It is a site of being.

The work done there is not about managing students through their “damage.” It is about making space where they can exist fully before the institution decides what they mean.

When a student’s poem or painting draws on sensory or ancestral knowledge that the academy does not authorize, she is not simply illustrating findings. She is producing knowledge that the institution cannot extinguish.

This is where my own framework begins. My dissertation argued that being, or ontology, must come before knowing. You cannot build legitimate knowledge on a foundation of suppressed being. The “archaeology of self” we ask of teachers is not a reflective exercise but the ontological work that must precede any claim to knowledge.

Land, Labour, and the Invisible Cost

By afternoon, the conversation turned to racial capitalism. The convenor invited us to move beyond economics and return to land.

Many frameworks start with capital and add race as a differentiating factor. This one reversed that order: land dispossession comes first.

The original accumulation that makes racial capitalism possible begins with the removal of Indigenous peoples from the relationships that sustain their cosmologies.

Through that lens, an international student paying triple tuition for a Western degree and a Cree community whose health knowledge is absorbed into a university research output experience different forms of the same foundational violence.

The student absorbs the risk and captures none of the value, but the deeper loss is ontological. The institution reshapes their world, not only what they know but how they can know. The credential becomes visible proof, while the cost is what can no longer be said in the language of their community because the institution has no category for it.

The Unreachable Remainder

We closed with an analysis of colonial law and constrained identity. The Indian Act remains the clearest example: a colonial state defining identity in racial terms to serve itself.

What cannot be administered or contained is the ontology.

The state can legislate who is legally Indigenous, but it cannot define a person’s relationship to land or to ceremony. These persist precisely because the institution, for all its bureaucratic precision, cannot reach where they live.

My own path through three decades of classrooms, from Ontario to the Cordillera mountains of Benguet, has been a slow recognition of what the institution was reaching for and what it could never touch.

It reached for procedure, curriculum guides, professional standards, and the supposed objectivity of the lab.

What it could not touch was the understanding I carried in my body from listening to Elders on Manitoulin Island, or from adult literacy students in a Philippine mountain village who taught crop rotation as reciprocity with the earth, not as resource management.

The institution will keep trying to administer the being of racialized and Indigenous students. It is skilled at this.

But it cannot reach the ground from which resistance and recovery grow.

That remainder, unreachable and unquantifiable, is where the work begins.
