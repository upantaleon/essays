When AI Learns to Reason, Who Gets Left Out?

June 25, 2026

The new paper on AMIE makes a strong case that conversational AI is getting better at disease management, not just diagnosis. It is also a reminder that technical progress can hide a basic political question: whose knowledge is being encoded, whose data is being used, and whose standards define “good care”? Nature, 2026

At first glance, this looks like a story about capability. AMIE was tested in a randomized, blinded virtual OSCE, compared with 21 primary care physicians, and reported to be non-inferior on management reasoning while outperforming physicians on several measures of preciseness and guideline alignment. That matters. But if we stop at performance, we miss the deeper issue: the study’s definition of legitimate knowledge is narrow, medically standardized, and institutionally centralized. Nature, 2026

That narrowing is where Indigenous knowledges enter the conversation.

Indigenous health traditions do not begin from the assumption that clinical guidelines are the highest form of truth. They begin from relationships: with land, community, family, memory, and collective responsibility. A system like AMIE may be able to reproduce evidence-based pathways, but that is not the same thing as understanding wellness in an Indigenous frame. If the model cannot recognize culturally grounded priorities, community-defined care, or the historical violence embedded in many health systems, then it is not neutral. It is reproducing a particular worldview and presenting it as universal.

The same logic applies to data sovereignty. AI systems are never just “using data.” They are extracting, organizing, and operationalizing it. In healthcare, that matters enormously, because data about patients is also data about power. Indigenous data sovereignty demands that Indigenous peoples control how data about them is collected, stored, interpreted, and reused. If those communities do not govern the process, then even the most sophisticated health AI risks repeating an old pattern: taking knowledge from marginalized communities and returning value elsewhere.

That is why the social justice stakes are not decorative. They are structural.

The paper presents AMIE as a potential tool for fragmented health systems, and that aspiration is understandable. Many communities face shortages of primary care, long waits, and uneven access to expertise. But a system that performs well inside a tightly controlled simulation is not automatically justice-enhancing in the real world. In fact, without explicit safeguards, it may deepen inequity by privileging cases, guidelines, and datasets that reflect high-resource clinical assumptions rather than the lived realities of Indigenous, racialized, rural, or otherwise underserved communities.

The study’s own design shows the limitation clearly. It uses simulated patient actors, a text-only interface, and cases designed with definitive answers for scoring reliability. That makes the experiment cleaner, but it also makes it less representative of actual care. Real patients are not standardized cases. Real care is messy, relational, and shaped by histories that cannot be reduced to a prompt and a guideline corpus. Indigenous care settings especially require attention to trust, sovereignty, and community context, not just accurate recommendations.

This is where the paper becomes useful beyond medicine. It reveals a broader problem in AI evaluation: systems are often judged by whether they can outperform professionals inside institutional norms, not by whether they can act responsibly across unequal social worlds. That is a much smaller question than the one policymakers and health systems should be asking. The right question is not whether AI can sound clinically competent. It is whether it can do so without overriding local knowledge, weakening community governance, or laundering structural injustice through technical sophistication.

If this research were redesigned through an Indigenous and social justice lens, the changes would be substantial. Indigenous communities would need to be co-designers, not just future beneficiaries. Data governance would need to follow Indigenous authority, not platform ownership. Evaluation would need to include cultural safety, relational accountability, and community-defined outcomes. And the system would need to recognize when guidelines are not enough, because good care is not always the same thing as standardized care.

That is the real lesson of the AMIE paper. The model may be advancing, but the politics of knowledge have not gone away. The more powerful these systems become, the more urgent it is to ask not just what they can do, but who they are built for, who controls them, and what they erase in the process.
